The harbour town keeps a small museum about its tides. In the first room a long wooden table holds a copy of the old tide charts, drawn by hand on heavy paper and weighted at the corners with smooth grey stones. Each chart shows a week of high and low water, with the times written in a careful script and the heights marked as short vertical lines. Visitors are invited to compare the charts with the brass gauge on the wall, which still rises and falls with the water in the channel outside. The second room describes how the charts were made: a keeper read the gauge every hour, wrote the reading in a ledger, and at the end of each month copied the ledger into a fair chart for the harbour office. Boats left and returned by those charts, and the fishing crews learned to read them before they learned to mend a net. A third room holds the tools of the work: a lantern with a cracked lens, a set of brass dividers, a stack of blank ledgers that were never used, and a barometer whose needle has stuck at the word change. The last room is a window. It looks out over the channel, where the water moves the same way it did when the first chart was drawn, twice in and twice out each day, a little later every evening than the evening before.
